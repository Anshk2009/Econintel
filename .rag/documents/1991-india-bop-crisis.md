The 1991 Indian balance-of-payments crisis was a severe external-sector crisis that pushed India to the brink of defaulting on its foreign obligations and directly triggered the country's economic liberalisation.

Key facts (verified):
- By January 1991, India's foreign exchange reserves had fallen to roughly USD 1.2 billion, and by June were depleted by about half — barely enough to cover around three weeks of essential imports.
- To raise emergency funds, the Reserve Bank of India airlifted gold reserves abroad: about 47 tonnes to the Bank of England and about 20 tonnes to the Union Bank of Switzerland, raising roughly USD 600 million. The 47-tonne airlift to the Bank of England occurred between 21 and 31 May 1991, during a general election.
- India secured emergency financing from the International Monetary Fund, pledging gold as collateral, and accepted a set of structural-reform conditions.
- The crisis precipitated the 1991 reforms (liberalisation, privatisation, globalisation) led by Finance Minister Manmohan Singh under PM Narasimha Rao.

Why it matters as an analogue: the canonical example of how a thin reserve buffer plus persistent current-account and fiscal deficits can force abrupt, externally-imposed reform. Useful when analysing emerging-market currency and reserve stress, especially in an Indian context.
