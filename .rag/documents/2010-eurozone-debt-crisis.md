The Eurozone sovereign debt crisis threatened the survival of the euro as Greece and other periphery states faced unsustainable borrowing costs.

Key facts (verified):
- Greece's budget deficit was revealed to exceed 12% of GDP (later revised to about 15.4%); rating agencies cut Greek debt to junk in early 2010.
- The IMF and EU agreed a first bailout of €110bn over three years, conditioned on harsh austerity (tax hikes, pension and public-wage cuts).
- An October 2011 plan delivered a second bailout (about $178bn) including a roughly 50% "voluntary" haircut for private holders of Greek debt.
- On 26 July 2012, ECB President Mario Draghi pledged to do "whatever it takes" to save the euro; the ECB launched Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT) in September 2012, sharply lowering periphery borrowing costs.
- Between 2011 and 2018 the EFSF/ESM lent about €300bn to Greece, Portugal, Ireland, Spain, and Cyprus.

Why it matters as an analogue: shows how a monetary union without fiscal union transmits sovereign stress, and how a credible central-bank backstop ("whatever it takes") can halt a self-fulfilling debt spiral.
