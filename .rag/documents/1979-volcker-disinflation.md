Fed Chairman Paul Volcker broke US double-digit inflation by raising interest rates to unprecedented levels, at the cost of a severe recession.

Key facts (verified):
- When Volcker became Fed chair in August 1979, inflation was about 9% and rising; CPI inflation peaked at about 14.8% in March 1980.
- On 6 October 1979 the Fed shifted to targeting nonborrowed reserves rather than the fed funds rate, letting rates rise without political constraint.
- The fed funds rate rose from about 11.4% (Oct 1979) toward about 17.6% by April 1980 and peaked around 22.4% in July 1981.
- The 1981–82 recession lasted 16 months (the longest postwar downturn until 2008); unemployment peaked at 10.8% in late 1982 and real GDP fell about 2.7% peak-to-trough.
- CPI inflation fell to about 6.2% by end-1982 and about 3.2% by 1983.

Why it matters as an analogue: the defining example of central-bank credibility and the real-economy cost of breaking entrenched inflation — directly relevant to any modern rate-hiking, inflation-fighting cycle.
