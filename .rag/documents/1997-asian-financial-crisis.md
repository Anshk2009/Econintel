The 1997 Asian financial crisis began with the collapse of the Thai baht and spread across East and Southeast Asia, forcing major IMF bailouts.

Key facts (verified):
- Thailand faced a speculative attack on the baht in May 1997; with limited FX reserves it could not defend its dollar peg and floated the baht on 2 July 1997, triggering a devaluation of roughly 70%.
- The baht fell from a peg of about 25 per dollar to about 56 per dollar by January 1998.
- Thailand accepted IMF loan conditions on 14 August 1997. The IMF and other creditors assembled bailouts of about $20bn for Thailand, $40bn for Indonesia, and $59bn for South Korea.
- Underlying causes: heavy short-term foreign borrowing, speculative investment, weak and over-leveraged financial systems, and fragile currency pegs.

Why it matters as an analogue: the classic case of a currency-peg collapse and capital-flight contagion across emerging markets — useful for analysing FX pegs, reserve adequacy, and regional financial contagion.
