UPI won. Now someone has to pay for it.

17 August 2026 · 7 min read · By Ansh

India's Unified Payments Interface now moves more than 23 billion payments a month and roughly half the world's real-time transaction volume. It does this while charging merchants nothing. The unresolved question is not whether UPI works — it plainly does — but who funds rails that get more expensive to run precisely as they succeed.

Ten years after launch, UPI is the most-cited success story in Indian public policy, and the citation is deserved. But the interesting part of the story is no longer adoption. It is the arithmetic underneath adoption, which points somewhere uncomfortable.

What actually changed in 2016

The common framing — that UPI made payments easier — understates it. Card networks already moved money electronically. What the National Payments Corporation of India built in April 2016 was different in kind: a public switch with an open API, sitting above the banks rather than inside any one of them.

Two design decisions did the real work. First, interoperability by default: any compliant app can move money between any two bank accounts, so a merchant's choice of provider stopped mattering to the customer. Second, the virtual payment address, which decoupled identity from account numbers and IFSC codes. Together they removed the coordination problem that had kept Indian digital payments locked inside individual bank apps and closed wallets.

Adoption followed the plumbing, not the marketing. The Jan Dhan account push gave people bank accounts, Aadhaar gave them verifiable identity, and cheap mobile data gave them a client. UPI supplied the missing switch. Demonetisation in November 2016 supplied the shove.

UPI's genuine innovation was institutional, not technical: it made the payment rail a public utility and left competition to the apps on top.

The number nobody puts in the press release

In FY 2025-26, UPI volume grew about 30% year-on-year while value grew about 20.6%. Those two figures do not describe the same trend. When transaction count outruns transaction value, the average payment is shrinking: 

FY2024-25   avg ticket  ≈  ₹1,401
FY2025-26   avg ticket  ≈  ₹1,300      (−7.2% YoY)
July 2026   avg ticket  ≈  ₹1,263

This is usually reported as good news, and in one sense it is: UPI has moved past replacing the monthly grocery bill and is now replacing coins — the ₹20 chai, the ₹60 auto fare. That is financial inclusion doing exactly what it was meant to do.

It is also, from the operator's side, a deteriorating business. The cost of running UPI is substantially per transaction — authorisation, switching, fraud checks, dispute handling, round-the-clock uptime. Industry estimates put the annual cost of the ecosystem near ₹20,000 crore. Spread across roughly 241 billion transactions in FY26, that is on the order of ₹0.83 per transaction.

Now hold that against a zero merchant discount rate. Revenue per transaction is fixed at nothing, cost per transaction is roughly constant, and the average ticket that any future percentage-based fee would be levied on is falling around 7% a year. Success makes the gap wider, not narrower.

Where this estimate is soft: the ₹20,000 crore figure is an industry aggregate, not an audited cost base, and per-transaction cost varies enormously between a large public-sector bank and a third-party app. Treat ₹0.83 as an order of magnitude, not a precise unit economic. The direction of travel is the robust part.

Two unresolved problems

Who pays. Zero MDR was a deliberate policy choice, following the Nilekani Committee's push to deepen digital payments, and it worked. But the subsidy appropriated to compensate banks and providers has consistently covered only a fraction of the estimated cost. The industry has therefore been funding a public utility out of its own balance sheet, cross-subsidised by lending and other fee income. That is stable only while the transaction count is small relative to the subsidy — which it no longer is. The reform debate that surfaced in August 2026 around reintroducing MDR for large merchants is the predictable consequence.

Who runs it. UPI's openness produced concentration anyway. PhonePe holds roughly 45.5% of volume and Google Pay roughly 34.6% — over 83% between two foreign-backed apps. NPCI's own 30% market-share cap has now been deferred a second time, to 31 December 2026, for the straightforward reason that enforcing it would require breaking the two apps most people actually use. A rule that cannot be enforced without disrupting four-fifths of the market is not really a rule yet.

The historical parallel

The closest analogue is not another payments launch but China between 2015 and 2021. Alipay and WeChat Pay achieved comparable ubiquity and a comparable duopoly, also on near-zero consumer pricing, also celebrated as an inclusion triumph. The resolution was not competitive entry. It was the state: the People's Bank of China moved payments onto a centralised clearing house, and the 2021 intervention in Ant Group forcibly restructured the largest player.

The instructive detail is the sequence. Concentration was tolerated while it delivered adoption, and became a systemic-risk question the moment adoption was finished. India is at that hinge now. The repeated deferral of the market-share cap is what tolerance looks like shortly before it ends.

Brazil's Pix is the useful contrast. It launched in 2020 with the same free, instant, interoperable design — but the central bank operates the rail directly rather than delegating to a member-owned corporation. Brazil consequently has a clearer answer to "who pays," because the answer is the state, explicitly.

What would change this view

  • Average ticket size stabilising or rising. If UPI starts carrying credit and higher-value commerce, the percentage-fee model becomes viable again and the cost squeeze eases on its own.
  • A funded subsidy that actually matches cost. The problem is not zero MDR as such; it is zero MDR with partial reimbursement. Full funding turns this into a straightforward public-goods expenditure.
  • Credible enforcement of the 30% cap, or its formal abandonment. Either resolves the ambiguity. A third deferral would tell you the constraint is not real.
  • Internationalisation revenue. Cross-border UPI acceptance carries genuine fees, which could cross-subsidise the domestic rail.

The honest summary is that UPI has comprehensively solved the problem it was built for and has not yet solved the problem that success created. Those are different problems, and the second one is harder, because it is distributional rather than technical. Someone has to pay ₹0.83, roughly 24 billion times a month, and India has not decided who.
